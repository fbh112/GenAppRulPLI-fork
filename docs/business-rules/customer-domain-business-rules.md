# Customer Domain — Business Rules

**Purpose**: Business rules the migration team must understand and preserve. These describe *what* the system does and *why* — independent of the PL/I implementation details.  
**Source programs**: [`LGACUS01`](../../pli/LGACUS01.pli) · [`LGACDB01`](../../pli/LGACDB01.pli) · [`LGACDB02`](../../pli/LGACDB02.pli) · [`LGICDB01`](../../pli/LGICDB01.pli) · [`LGUCDB01`](../../pli/LGUCDB01.pli)

> **Scope note**: Implementation-level concerns (COMMAREA length checks, CICS abend codes, VSAM ordering, DB2 SQLCODE mappings) are documented separately in [`customer-domain-migration-analysis.md`](../architecture/customer-domain-migration-analysis.md).

## BR-01 · Every Customer Has a Permanent, Immutable Identity Number

A customer is assigned a unique system-wide identity number at the moment of creation. This number:
- Is never reused, even if a customer record is subsequently deleted
- Is the single key used by all downstream systems (policies, claims, billing) to reference this customer
- Is generated by the system — callers do not supply it
- Is returned to the caller synchronously as part of the creation response

**Why it matters**: Every policy, claim, and financial record in the system references the customer number. Any migration that changes the numbering scheme, allows gaps or reuse, or makes the number non-deterministic will silently corrupt cross-system references.

## BR-02 · Every New Customer Must Have a Security Credential Record

Creating a customer is a two-step atomic operation: the personal record is created, and a security credential record is created in the same transaction. A customer record cannot exist without a corresponding credential record.

The initial credential state is:
- Password: set to a default system-issued value (not customer-chosen)
- Status: **New / Not Yet Activated** — the customer has not yet set their own password
- Change count: zero

**Why it matters**: The security record is the gateway for the customer to authenticate. A customer without a credential record cannot be activated. The atomicity requirement means partial creation (customer record exists, security record does not) must be treated as a system failure and rolled back — it must never be left as a valid state.

## BR-03 · A Customer's Personal Details Comprise Exactly Nine Fields

A customer record holds a fixed, well-defined set of personal information. No more, no fewer:

| Field | Business meaning |
|---|---|
| First name | Customer's given name (up to 10 characters) |
| Last name | Customer's family name (up to 20 characters) |
| Date of birth | Used for identity verification and age-related eligibility checks |
| House name | Named property identifier (where applicable) |
| House number | Street number |
| Post code | Geographic location for pricing and regulatory purposes |
| Mobile phone | Primary contact number |
| Home phone | Secondary contact number |
| Email address | Digital contact and notification channel |

None of these fields carry format or content validation in the current system — the data is stored as supplied. The migration is an opportunity to introduce validation (e.g. postcode format, email format), but this is a new capability, not a preservation requirement.

**Why it matters**: Existing callers always supply all nine fields. An update replaces the entire record — there is no concept of changing a single field while leaving others unchanged. Any new service that introduces partial update must explicitly co-ordinate this as a breaking change with all callers.

## BR-04 · A Customer's Policy Count Is Not Owned by the Customer Domain

When a customer record is retrieved, the number of policies held by that customer is always returned as zero. The Customer domain does not maintain or calculate policy counts — that information belongs to the Policy domain.

**Why it matters**: A migrated Customer service must not fabricate policy counts. If a client needs to display the number of policies a customer holds, it must make a separate call to the Policy service. Combining this into the Customer service would create a hidden cross-domain dependency.

---

## BR-05 · Deleting a Customer Cascades to All Their Policies

There is currently no supported Delete Customer operation. However, the data model enforces a cascade rule: if a customer record is deleted, all policies belonging to that customer are automatically deleted, along with all associated policy-type records (motor, endowment, house, commercial) and claims.

**Why it matters**: The cascade is silent and irreversible. Any future implementation of customer deletion must be a deliberate, co-ordinated action that:
1. Confirms with the Policy domain that the customer holds no active policies, or
2. Explicitly deactivates or transfers all policies before deleting the customer

A simple delete without this co-ordination will silently destroy policy and claims data owned by other domains.

---

## BR-06 · A Policy Can Only Be Created for an Existing Customer

When a policy is created and linked to a customer, the system enforces that the customer must already exist. Attempting to create a policy for a non-existent customer is rejected with a specific error — it is not silently ignored or auto-corrected.

**Why it matters**: In a distributed architecture, the Customer service and Policy service may be separate systems. The Policy service must still enforce referential integrity at the application level, since there will no longer be a database-level foreign key constraint guaranteeing this. The check "does this customer exist before creating their policy?" must be an explicit step in the policy creation flow.

---

## BR-07 · Deleting a Policy Is an Idempotent Operation

Requesting the deletion of a policy that does not exist is treated as a success. The business outcome ("this policy no longer exists") is the same whether or not the record was present before the request.

**Why it matters**: This is a deliberate design choice that simplifies retry logic and error recovery for callers. Any migrated implementation must preserve this behaviour — a caller that retries a failed delete must not receive an error on the second attempt.

---

## BR-08 · Policy Timestamps Are Authoritative and System-Assigned

When a policy is created, the `lastChanged` timestamp is set by the database at the moment of write — it is never supplied by the caller. After creation, the actual server-assigned timestamp is returned to the caller.

**Why it matters**: The timestamp is used to detect concurrent modifications and to audit the history of policy changes. If the application clock were used instead of the database clock, distributed deployments across multiple application servers could produce inconsistent or out-of-order timestamps. The database must remain the authoritative source for this value.
